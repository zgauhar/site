Timeboxing means giving a task a fixed block of time in advance, starting a countdown timer, and stopping or reviewing when it rings. Instead of working until a task feels done, you decide how much time it deserves and fit the work into that box. It works because the deadline is real and visible, which stops small jobs swelling and makes big ones easier to start.

Why a time limit beats "work until it is done"

Work expands to fill the time available. An open-ended email reply can take ten minutes or an hour, and without a limit you will not notice which. A box also makes starting easier: "twenty minutes on the tax forms" feels smaller than "do the tax forms".

How to choose the length of a timebox

Type of taskSuggested box
Dreaded or tiny jobs, admin15 to 25 minutes
Email and messages15 to 30 minutes, once or twice a day
Focused writing, coding, studying45 to 90 minutes
Meetings and callsFixed by the agenda, with a countdown for each item
Open-ended thinking25 to 45 minutes, then review what you have

If you are unsure, start shorter than you think. You can always book a second box.

How to timebox a task in five steps

  1. Define the outcome. "Draft the intro" works. "Work on the report" does not.
  2. Estimate and shrink it. Guess how long it needs, then cut it by a quarter to push yourself to the essentials.
  3. Start a countdown. A visible timer beats a mental note, and the alarm saves you watching the clock.
  4. Work on that one thing. Park distractions on a list instead of switching.
  5. When the timer rings, stop and decide. Done, another box, or smaller task.

Common timeboxing mistakes

  • Boxes that are too long. Past 90 minutes focus drops. Split the work.
  • No breaks between boxes. Leave five to ten minutes to move, drink water and reset.
  • Quietly extending. If you always add "just five more minutes", the box has no effect. Make extending a decision.
  • Boxing everything. Use it on tasks you avoid or that tend to overrun. Creative flow does not need a cut-off.
  • Setting an impossible box. If a task always overruns, your estimate is wrong. Note the real time and use it next time.

Timeboxing versus the Pomodoro technique

Pomodoro is a special case: always 25 minutes of work and 5 of rest. Timeboxing lets the length follow the task, so a 15-minute inbox box and a 60-minute writing box can sit in the same day.
