A sinking fund is money you put aside every month for a cost you know is coming, such as Christmas, car insurance or a yearly subscription. Divide the expected cost by the months until it is due, set that amount aside each payday, and the bill stops being a shock. Below you will find a list of common irregular expenses, a worked example, what to do if you start late and how sinking funds fit into a daily budget.

Why irregular expenses break budgets

Most budgets plan for rent, bills and groceries, the costs that come every month. The money problems usually come from costs that arrive once or twice a year: the car service, the insurance renewal, birthdays, Christmas. None of them is a real surprise, but because they are not monthly they never make it into the plan. A sinking fund turns each of them into a small, predictable monthly amount.

Go through the last 12 months of bank statements and note every cost that did not come monthly. That list is your starting point.

How to work out the monthly amount

  1. Name each fund and its cost. For example Christmas 600, car insurance 480, car service 240, annual subscriptions 120.
  2. Find the months until each one is due. If a cost is a year away, use 12.
  3. Divide the cost by the months. That is the monthly contribution.
  4. Add the contributions up and treat the total as one monthly bill.

Starting late, for example in November

If Christmas is about six weeks away and you have saved nothing, the maths is less kind: 600 over 1.5 months is 400 a month. You have three options, and most people use a mix:

  • Lower the target. Decide on a smaller amount now rather than finding out in January.
  • Cut wants temporarily. Take the difference out of eating out, shopping and other flexible spending until the date.
  • Start next year's fund in January. 50 a month from January makes next December easy.

Avoid putting the gap on a credit card. If you do, add the repayment to your budget as a bill straight away.

Sinking fund vs emergency fund

A sinking fund is for costs you can predict, even if they are irregular. An emergency fund is for things you cannot predict, such as losing your job or a burst pipe. Keep the two apart, so Christmas never eats the money meant for a real emergency. One savings account can hold both if you track how much belongs to each.

How sinking funds fit into a daily budget

A daily spending limit only works if it already accounts for the money you are setting aside. In Daily Budget, add your monthly sinking fund total as a bill, so the daily allowance is worked out from what is left after it. When the expense finally comes, mark it as paid from savings, and it does not take today's allowance. With Pro, savings goals let you track each fund separately.
