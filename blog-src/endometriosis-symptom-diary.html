Endometriosis and adenomyosis are hard to explain in a ten-minute appointment. Pain changes from day to day, symptoms are spread across the body, and on a good day it is easy to forget how bad the last bad one was. A symptom diary gives you dates and numbers instead of "it's been a rough few months".

This article is general information, not medical advice. A diary helps you describe what you experience; it cannot diagnose anything. Talk to your doctor about your symptoms and what to do about them.

Why a diary helps

  • It shows patterns. Pain that follows your cycle looks different from pain that does not, and you can only see that over several months.
  • It makes the impact visible. Missed work, days in bed and cancelled plans are part of the picture, and they are easy to undercount from memory.
  • It gives a before and after. When you start, change or stop a treatment, dated entries show what moved and what did not.
  • It saves appointment time. A summary you can hand over lets the conversation start with the facts.

What is worth logging

1. Pain, as a number

Rate your worst pain of the day from 0 to 10. A number is quick, and it can be compared month to month. Decide what the numbers mean to you and keep it consistent. A common convention is that 4 and above interferes with your day and 7 and above is severe.

2. Where and what kind

Note the location (lower abdomen on the left or right, lower back, thighs, rectal pain) and the type: cramping, stabbing, burning, aching or radiating. Doctors ask about both, and "lower left, stabbing" is more useful than "pain".

3. The specific pains doctors ask about

Endometriosis is often asked about in separate parts: pain during your period, pain during or after sex, pain with bowel movements, pain when urinating and pelvic pain outside your period. Scoring each from 0 to 10 now and then, even weekly, covers the questions you are most likely to be asked.

4. Bleeding

Record bleeding from spotting to very heavy. It places pain inside your cycle and shows changes in cycle length or flow.

5. Other symptoms

Bloating, fatigue, nausea, bowel and bladder symptoms, headaches, brain fog and low mood all belong. They are often the symptoms that get dropped from the story because they feel unrelated.

6. Medication and what helped

Log what you took and roughly when, and whether it helped. This is what a doctor needs to judge whether a treatment is doing its job.

7. How the day went

A simple yes or no for missed work, worked through pain, stayed in bed, cancelled plans or went to the emergency room. These are the entries that tell the real story, and they matter for sick-leave conversations and benefit assessments.

8. Possible triggers

Sleep, stress, food and activity are worth tracking if you have the energy, with a caution. A single bad day after a particular food proves nothing. Patterns only mean something after weeks of entries, so treat them as questions to take to your doctor, not conclusions.

Keep it small on bad days

The diary that works is the one you actually fill in. On a bad day, log the pain number and nothing else, and add detail later or not at all. A gap in the diary is fine. What matters is that on most days there is at least one number. Log at the same time every day, for example with a reminder, so it becomes routine rather than a decision.

Turning the diary into an appointment summary

A pile of daily entries is hard to read in a short appointment. Before you go, boil the last one to three months down to a page:

  1. How many days had pain, and how many were severe.
  2. When in the cycle the pain was worst.
  3. Which of the five pain types above stood out.
  4. How many days affected work or daily life.
  5. What you took, and how often.
  6. If you changed treatment, how the weeks before and after compare.
  7. Your questions, written down before you walk in.

Bring it printed or on your phone, and offer it at the start. It is your account, in your words and numbers, and it does not replace the examination.

Keep your health data private

A symptom diary is sensitive. Before choosing a notebook, spreadsheet or app, check where the entries are stored, whether there is an account, whether anything is shared with advertisers or analytics services, and whether you can export and delete everything. On paper, a notebook in a drawer is private by default. In an app, look for one that keeps entries on your device.
