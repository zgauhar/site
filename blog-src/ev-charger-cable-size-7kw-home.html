A 7.4 kW home EV charger is a 32 A single-phase load, and on a short run clipped to a wall it needs a 4 mm² copper cable. Put the cable in an insulated wall and it goes up to 6 mm²; stretch the run to 30 m and it is 10 mm² either way. The rating of the cable, the breaker and the voltage drop all have to pass, and the biggest result sets the size.

Why you size for the rated current, not the power

A charger draws its full rated current for hours at a time. IEC 60364-7-722 therefore treats it as a continuous load: size the circuit for the charger's rated current, with no diversity allowed. A 7.4 kW charger on 230 V is 32 A (7,400 ÷ 230 ≈ 32). If the charger lets you set a lower limit such as 16 A, size for the setting you will really use, and remember someone may raise it later.

Three-phase chargers need far thinner cable for the same power because each conductor carries less current and the voltage-drop factor is lower. The numbers come from IEC 60364-5-52 Annex B capacities with temperature and grouping factors applied; a hot garage or several circuits in one conduit can push you up a size.

The three checks behind the table

  1. Current-carrying capacity. After derating, the cable must carry at least the breaker rating. Insulated walls (A1) shed heat badly, which is why the same 32 A needs 6 mm² there but only 4 mm² when clipped to a wall. See how to choose a breaker size for a cable.
  2. Breaker. The smallest standard rating at or above 32 A is 32 A. The rule is IB ≤ In ≤ Iz.
  3. Voltage drop. Long garden or driveway runs are where cable size jumps. 32 A over 30 m needs 10 mm² because of the drop, not the current. The maths is in voltage drop: what to do when the cable is too long.

What else to check before installing

  • A dedicated circuit for each charging point.
  • RCD protection for each charging point, with DC fault detection: often a type A RCD plus 6 mA DC detection in the charger, or a type B RCD. The charger's manual says which.
  • Buried or outdoor runs. Direct-buried cable (method D) and cable in direct sun use other factors than the table above.
  • Your supply. The main fuse and the rest of the house load need headroom, and many chargers can limit their current to protect the main fuse.
  • Local rules. National wiring rules may be stricter, and the installer remains responsible for the design.
