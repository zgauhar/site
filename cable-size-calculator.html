How the size is chosen

  1. Breaker. The smallest standard rating In at or above the load current Ib (6, 10, 13, 16, 20, 25, 32, 40, 50, 63 A …).
  2. Current capacity. The smallest cable whose tabulated capacity, multiplied by the temperature and grouping factors, is at least In. That gives Ib ≤ In ≤ Iz.
  3. Voltage drop. The smallest cable whose drop over the length stays within your limit (IEC 60364-5-52 Annex G).

The recommended size is the larger of steps 2 and 3, and the calculator tells you which one decided it.

Worked example

A 20 A single-phase circuit, 15 m of PVC cable clipped direct (method C), 30 °C, on its own: the breaker is 20 A and 2.5 mm² carries 27 A, which is enough. Its drop over 15 m is 2.3%, inside a 3% limit, so the answer is 2.5 mm². Bunch it with three other circuits at 35 °C and the factors (0.94 × 0.65) cut the capacity, so it needs 4 mm². Leave it on its own but make the run 45 m and the voltage drop decides instead: 6 mm² (current alone would need 2.5 mm²).

What size cable for 16, 20, 32 or 40 amps?

Smallest copper size that carries the breaker rating, for PVC cable at 30 °C with no other circuits grouped. These are current capacity only: long runs need a bigger size for voltage drop, and heat or grouping change them, so use the calculator for your case.

Installation methods

  • A1: single cores in conduit in an insulated wall
  • A2: multi-core cable in conduit in an insulated wall
  • B1: single cores in conduit on a wall
  • B2: multi-core cable in conduit on a wall
  • C: cable clipped direct to a wall
  • E: multi-core cable in free air or on a perforated tray

A cable surrounded by thermal insulation (A1, A2) can't shed heat, so it carries the least. In free air (E) it carries the most.

Questions

What does derating mean?

The tabulated capacity assumes 30 °C air and one circuit on its own. Hotter air or several loaded circuits bunched together stop the cable losing heat, so the capacity is multiplied by correction factors below 1 and a bigger size may be needed.

Why is the breaker bigger than my load?

Breakers come in standard ratings, so a 14 A load gets a 16 A breaker. The cable is then sized to carry the breaker rating, not just the load, so the breaker protects it against overload (Ib ≤ In ≤ Iz).

Which standard does the calculator use?

IEC 60364-5-52: Annex B for current-carrying capacity and Annex G for voltage drop, for copper conductors from 1.5 to 120 mm². It is not built for the US NEC or AWG sizes.

Can I rely on the result without checking?

No. It is guidance based on IEC 60364. Always check against the standard, the cable manufacturer's data and your national wiring rules. The installer remains responsible for the design.
