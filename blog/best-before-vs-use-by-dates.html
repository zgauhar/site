Best before vs use by: what food date labels really mean

"Use by" is a safety date and "best before" is a quality date. Food past its use by date should not be eaten, even if it looks and smells fine. Food past its best before date is usually still safe, it just may not taste or feel as good. Mixing the two up is the most common reason people bin good food or, worse, eat food that is not safe.

Best before vs use by at a glance

Use byBest before
What it is aboutFood safetyQuality (taste, texture, colour)
Typical foodsFresh meat and fish, ready meals, chilled salads, some dairyTins, pasta, rice, biscuits, frozen food, many long-life products
After the dateDo not eat or cook itUsually safe, check it looks and smells right
Can you freeze it?Yes, up to the use by date, then follow the pack's instructionsYes, before or after the date

The exact wording differs by country. In the UK and EU, "use by" and "best before" work as above. In the US, labels such as "sell by", "best if used by" and "use by" are mostly quality or stock-rotation guides, with infant formula being the main case where dates are about safety. When in doubt, check the guidance from your local food safety agency.

What to do when a food date has passed

  1. Use by date passed: throw it away. Harmful bacteria can grow without changing the look or smell.
  2. Best before date passed: look, smell and, if the food is something you would normally taste, try a small amount. Dry goods, tins and frozen food are often fine for a long time. Anything mouldy, swollen, leaking or with a bad smell goes in the bin.
  3. Not sure which date it is: read the words, not the position on the pack. Treat anything unclear on fresh meat, fish or ready meals as a use by date.

Opened food has a different clock

The printed date only applies to the sealed, correctly stored product. Once you open it, the label often says something like "use within 3 days of opening" and that shorter limit wins. Cooked leftovers also have their own short life in the fridge. The easiest way to follow this is to note the date you opened the pack, and use whichever deadline comes first.

Other dates worth tracking

Food is only part of it. Several things around the home carry expiry dates that are easy to miss:

  • Medicines: out-of-date tablets and liquids should go back to a pharmacy. Many have a shorter life once opened, such as eye drops.
  • Cosmetics and sunscreen: look for the open-jar symbol, which shows how many months the product keeps after opening.
  • Documents: passports, ID cards, driving licences, visas and insurance cover. A passport that expires in a few weeks can stop a trip.
  • Warranties and subscriptions: returns windows and free trials run out quietly.

How to track expiry dates and waste less food

  1. Put the oldest at the front. Move new shopping behind older items in the fridge and cupboard.
  2. Keep a list of what expires soon. A weekly look at what is close to its date beats finding it at the back of the fridge.
  3. Set reminders ahead of the date. A reminder a week before leaves time to cook it, freeze it or give it away. For documents, 30 days or more gives time to renew.
  4. Freeze before the use by date. Bread, meat and cooked meals freeze well if you act in time.
  5. Record what happened. Noting what you used and what you threw away shows which foods you keep over-buying.
