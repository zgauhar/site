What size cable for an electric shower? 7.5 to 10.5 kW

An electric shower is a 33 to 46 A single-phase load, and the cable size follows from that current. A 7.5 or 8.5 kW shower clipped to a wall needs 6 mm² copper on a 40 A breaker. A 9.5 or 10.5 kW shower needs 10 mm² on a 50 A breaker, and 16 mm² if the cable runs through an insulated wall. Long runs push the size up because of voltage drop.

Electric shower current: kW to amps

A shower heater is a resistive load, so the current is simply the power divided by the voltage. At 230 V:

ShowerCurrent at 230 VBreaker (IEC series)
7.5 kW32.6 A40 A
8.5 kW37.0 A40 A
9.5 kW41.3 A50 A
10.5 kW45.7 A50 A

Shower ratings are often quoted at 240 V, where the same heater uses slightly more power. Use the voltage your supply actually has and the current in the installation manual if it gives one.

Capacities are from IEC 60364-5-52 Annex B: 6 mm² carries 46 A clipped direct but only 34 A in an insulated wall, and 10 mm² carries 63 A clipped direct but 46 A in an insulated wall. At 40 m a 10.5 kW shower needs 16 mm² even when clipped direct, because the voltage drop on 10 mm² passes 3%.

The 45 A breaker case

In countries where 45 A breakers are standard, such as the UK, 9.5 and 10.5 kW showers usually sit on 45 A instead of 50 A. 10 mm² in an insulated wall is rated 46 A, so it passes with 1 A to spare. Any derating, such as a hot loft, a cable bunched with others or thermal insulation around it, pushes it to 16 mm². Check the real route, not just the length.

The three checks behind every row

  1. Current-carrying capacity. After temperature and grouping factors, the cable must carry at least the breaker rating. This is why the insulated-wall rows jump a size. See how to choose a breaker size for a cable.
  2. Breaker. The rule is IB ≤ In ≤ Iz: shower current, then breaker, then cable capacity, each at least as big as the last.
  3. Voltage drop. On long runs from the consumer unit to an upstairs bathroom the drop, not the current, sets the size. The maths is in voltage drop: what to do when the cable is too long.

Before you swap or install a shower

  • Dedicated circuit. An electric shower always has its own circuit from the consumer unit.
  • RCD protection. Bathroom circuits need 30 mA RCD protection under IEC 60364-7-701.
  • Isolator. A double-pole isolating switch rated for the shower current, outside the zones or as a pull cord.
  • Upgrading the shower. A bigger shower on the old cable is the classic mistake. Recheck capacity, breaker and drop with the new current.
  • Your supply. A 10.5 kW shower plus an EV charger and an oven can exceed a small main fuse. Similar sizing for chargers is in what size cable for a 7.4 kW home EV charger.
  • Local rules. National wiring rules may be stricter, and the installer remains responsible for the design.

Frequently asked questions

What size cable for a 9.5 kW electric shower?

A 9.5 kW shower on 230 V draws about 41 A. Clipped direct to a wall (method C) on runs up to about 30 m it needs 10 mm² copper cable. In an insulated wall (method A1) on a 50 A breaker it needs 16 mm².

Is 6 mm² cable enough for an electric shower?

Only for smaller showers. 6 mm² clipped direct carries 46 A, so it works for a 7.5 or 8.5 kW shower on a 40 A breaker on a short run. Over about 25 m, or in an insulated wall, the same shower needs 10 mm².

What breaker size for an electric shower?

The smallest standard rating at or above the shower current: 40 A for 7.5 and 8.5 kW, and 50 A for 9.5 and 10.5 kW on the IEC series. Where 45 A breakers are standard, such as in the UK, 9.5 and 10.5 kW showers are usually on 45 A.

Can I fit a bigger shower on my existing cable?

Only if the existing cable still passes all three checks with the new current: capacity after derating, the breaker rule Ib ≤ In ≤ Iz, and voltage drop. Swapping an 8.5 kW shower on 6 mm² for a 10.5 kW one usually means a new cable and breaker.
