Endometriosis flare-ups: how to track triggers and what helped

To understand your endometriosis flare-ups, log each one with its start, its end, the worst pain from 0 to 10, the day of your cycle, what was going on beforehand and what helped. Flares often feel random, but after two or three cycles of notes many people see a pattern, such as flares around ovulation, after bad sleep or after certain foods, which you can plan around and discuss with your doctor.

This article is general information, not medical advice. A flare log helps you describe what you experience; it cannot diagnose anything or replace care. Talk to your doctor about your symptoms and your treatment.

What counts as a flare

A flare is a spell that is clearly worse than your normal days: stronger pelvic or back pain, more bloating, fatigue, nausea, or bowel and bladder symptoms. Decide your own threshold, for example "pain of 6 or more, or a day I cannot do my normal plans", and use it every time. A fixed rule keeps the log consistent. On a bad day, write the start time and the pain number and nothing else. You can fill in the rest when the flare has eased.

Commonly reported triggers

People with endometriosis often name these, but the evidence for most of them is limited and everyone is different. Treat the list as things to check in your own log, not rules:

  • the period itself and the days around ovulation;
  • stress and poor or short sleep;
  • certain foods, alcohol or caffeine, especially for bloating and bowel symptoms;
  • constipation or bowel changes;
  • intense exercise, or long periods sitting still;
  • sex, or a pelvic examination;
  • missed, late or changed medication.

Avoid cutting out whole food groups on a hunch. If your log points to food, bring it to your doctor or a dietitian.

How to spot your own pattern

  1. Track for at least two or three cycles. One flare after a glass of wine proves nothing; four out of five does say something.
  2. Lay the flares over your cycle. If most start on the same cycle days, that is worth raising with your doctor, because hormonal treatment options depend on it.
  3. Look at the days before each flare, not just the flare day itself.
  4. Check what helped, so you have a plan ready the next time: what to take, when, and what to cancel.

When to get urgent help

Do not wait for the log in these cases. Get urgent medical help for sudden severe pain that is new or different, fever, fainting, very heavy bleeding, vomiting that will not stop, or pain that your usual plan does not touch.

Frequently asked questions

What is an endometriosis flare-up?

A flare-up is a spell of symptoms that are clearly worse than your usual baseline, such as stronger pelvic pain, back pain, bloating, fatigue or bowel and bladder symptoms. It can last hours or several days and may or may not line up with your period.

What can trigger an endometriosis flare?

Commonly reported triggers include the period and ovulation, stress, poor sleep, some foods or alcohol, constipation, intense exercise, sex, and missed or changed medication. Evidence for most of them is limited and they differ from person to person, so tracking your own pattern is more useful than a general list.

How long does an endometriosis flare last?

It varies a lot: from a few hours to a week or more. Logging the start and end of each flare tells you what is typical for you, which helps you plan and gives your doctor a concrete picture.

When should I get urgent help during a flare?

Get urgent medical help for sudden severe pain that is new or different, fever, fainting, very heavy bleeding, vomiting that will not stop, or pain that your usual plan does not touch. A flare log is not a reason to wait.
