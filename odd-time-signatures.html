Odd time signatures: how to count 5/4, 7/8 and 9/8

Odd meters are just short and long groups. Learn the groupings, hear them on the click track below, and practise until the long group stops tripping you up.

How to practise

  1. Say the groups. Count 7/8 as "1-2, 1-2, 1-2-3" before you play a note.
  2. Clap only the accents with the click, then fill in the other notes.
  3. Go slow. 60–80 BPM until you can play eight clean bars; then add 5 BPM.
  4. Feel the long group. Most players rush the "3" at the end of 7/8 and 9/8. Leave it its full length.

Questions

What is an odd time signature?

A meter whose bar can't be split into equal groups of two or three beats, such as 5/4, 7/8 or 9/8 (as 2+2+2+3). They're counted as a mix of short (2) and long (3) groups.

How do you count 7/8?

Usually as 2+2+3: "1-2, 1-2, 1-2-3" or "1 & 2 & 3 & a". Other groupings such as 3+2+2 and 2+3+2 exist; the music tells you which one is meant.

Is 6/8 an odd time signature?

No. 6/8 is compound duple: two groups of three eighth notes, felt as two beats. It's included here because it's often practised alongside 5/8 and 7/8.

How should I practise odd meters?

Start slow (60–80 BPM), count the groups out loud, clap or tap the accents, then speed up only when you can play several clean bars in a row. Rhythm Trainer scores each tap to the millisecond so you can see if you're rushing the short groups.
